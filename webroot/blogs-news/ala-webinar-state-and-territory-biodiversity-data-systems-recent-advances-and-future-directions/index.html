ALA webinar: state and territory biodiversity data systems – recent advances and future directions.

Posted on 20th November 2026
We invited representatives from Dandjoo in Western Australia, NSW BioNet and the Natural Values Atlas in Tasmania to showcase their respective repositories, recent advances and future directions.

Australia is one of the most biologically diverse places on Earth. From our larger states, like Western Australia and New South Wales, to our smallest, Tasmania, each has high levels of regional endemism with species which occur nowhere else.

All the states and territories have publicly available atlases of their biodiversity data, and they share versions of this data with the Atlas of Living Australia. Data from states and territories makes up around 20% of the biodiversity data in Australia. It’s particularly important for our national understanding of vertebrate animals and vascular plants.

The conservation agencies in Australia’s states and territories manage large biodiversity data holdings for a wide variety of purposes centred upon environmental management and approvals and meeting statutory obligations under the various pieces of legislation in their jurisdictions.

The ALA receives support from the Australian Government through the National Collaborative Research Infrastructure Strategy (NCRIS) and is hosted by CSIRO.

Trisha Moriarty and Anshu Gupta

Dandjoo, Western Australian Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (DBCA), Biodiversity Information Office (BIO)

Ron Avery

NSW BioNet, Senior Team Leader, Biodiversity Information Systems,

David McElwee

Natural Values Atlas, Tasmania. Department of Natural Resources and Environment Tasmania

Facilitator

Peggy Newman
Team Data Management Lead, Atlas of Living Australia

Peggy is the Data Manager at the ALA, and the Australian Node Manager of the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). She leads the Data Management team who map, ingest and index datasets from hundreds of data providers from the government, collections and citizen science sectors.

Q&A discussion

The conversation sparked so many great questions – more than we could fit into the session, so we’ve taken the time to answer the rest here.

State and transition simulation models are more likely to be used with vegetation coverage datasets than the biodiversity occurrence datasets. We’ve discussed at this webinar.

In the ALA, sensitive species locations are systematically obscured for all ingested data using generalisation rules captured in Sensitive Lists (see: https://lists.ala.org.au/?filters=isSDS:true) provided by states and territories, and Birdlife Australia. Culturally sensitive species locations are managed through data governance processes and decisions made together with data providers.

In the ALA using Darwin Core, data providers may use the occurrenceStatus field to nominate “present” or “absent” values for an occurrence.

Species occurrence data exists for many islands; however, the ALA does not hold extensive data on whether a species is flagged as invasive for a particular location. To study invasive species in an area, we recommend uploading a list of your target invasive species to the lists tool and using it to query occurrences.

Yes, Biodiversity Information Office (Dandjoo) is planning to look at IMSA data. The initial focus has been on terrestrial data from the IBSA portal with survey data from 356 projects ingested. Dandjoo currently holds marine data from providers such WA Museum, iNaturalist and Dept. Biodiversity Conservation and Attractions.

BioNet contains marine and aquatic species data but this data holding is not comprehensive

a. Does it mean that eventually there will be only one ALA site with appropriate state management.

b. Also for the future is there any standardisation of AI platforms used?

Each state provided budget information in a previous question.

In the foreseeable future it is unlikely that all state and territory atlases and the ALA will be merged, as each has differing jurisdictional responsibilities and funding arrangements.

To that end there is no standardisation of AI platforms.

a. Is there an alternative to digital systems (i.e. if systems go down)?

b. Who owns this data?

Data in the ALA, and on most modern government platforms, is managed onshore either on premise or in the cloud. Non sensitive data that is published through the ALA is also published to the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF).

Most modern IT systems are well managed for resilience to outages and disaster recovery.

Most of the data we discussed in the webinar is released publicly by data providers under Creative Commons licensing, and they would be the most likely to be named the “data owners”. With biodiversity data, there are often many parties involved with the collection and dissemination of data (data custodians, stewards, informaticians) and it’s important that users of data correctly follow the attribution requirements as set out by the Creative Commons license applied by the data provider.

Data flows from BioNet to the ALA and to GBIF, and there will be differences in the data primarily due to the different taxonomic backbones of all three. The ALA invites new data providers and provides tooling (Galaxias) and guidance for sharing data.

The new NVA interface will be live on 19 November.

BioNet will be publishing its large flora and fauna systematic survey data holding via a new web service in 2026. This data is complex containing information about the site, replicates, survey method, species census, and measurements for fact. This is the first step in a longer journey of refinement. To interpret species absence routinely from these datasets further work may be needed. Site locations will be made available as spatial layer.

In the NVA survey effort should be captured in the survey metadata for each survey. It isn’t a map of survey effort (hours per grid square). I appreciate that would be nice, but it isn’t what we are showing. It is a step in the direction that would get us to that destination though.

a. That’s a great point. And it would be great if some information on survey effort/coverage would be given in these databases. In my experience, the “survey sites” names that I’ve seen in downloaded data from BioNet and NVA seem to be a mix of established survey sites and incidental records or unknown survey effort/coverage, and where possible, I have used other fields to determine the type of record (incidental, standardised survey, etc.). And absolutely, absences are often not captured or captured in an inconsistent way. But I would love to hear from the BioNet and NVA teams here!

b. Thanks. It seems that survey effort is only captured in metadata for species presence records. I once tried to look at all small mammal records, and plot any that had ‘mammal trapping’ as a method to understand spatial coverage of targeted small mammal surveys. roundabout way to get there! Would be great if it was captured

In general, we accept that systematic survey data needs to be a focus of our systems.

BioNet delivers species observation data to ALA and Biodiversity Data Repository, but does not yet received data back, although this is evolving as a priority. Species absence is not codified; it is inferred from other information including method, effort, and timing of survey that is held in the systematic survey data model and metadata. Exposure of this systematic survey data via open web services is a first step and will allow us to test and refine our approach in future releases.

Dandjoo delivers species occurrence and systematic survey data to the federal Biodiversity Data Repository and non-sensitive data is also available through ALA. Most data in Dandjoo have a Creative Commons By Attribution licence, but there are small number of datasets that cannot be on shared due to licence constraints impose by the rightsholder.

The NVA has generally not recorded absences for things that were not previously known to be present on that site—giant trees, eagle nests etc might be recorded as absent; shore birds, wallabies, ephemeral plants et cetera would not.

There is also a difference between ‘absent’ and ‘not detected’. We are hoping to accommodate both.

Going forwards, we should be doing a better job of capturing presences and not detected/absence data in the new NVA, but it is not something that was well catered for in the old NVA as recording of survey method and effort were not well supported and without that these are only half the whole.

a. I was wondering how frequently BioNet data is uploaded to ALA. Also does all data feed to ALA or subset of data

All publicly available BioNet data (a subset of the total) is loaded to the ALA weekly.

Records in the ALA are reprocessed regularly to match the provided taxonomic details (name, higher taxonomic details) to the current accepted taxonomic name. Historical views of the taxonomic backbone are not retained.
